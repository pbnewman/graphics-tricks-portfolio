The Doom fire effect

Each pixel reads from the row below with random horizontal jitter; a coin flip dims it by one palette index. Bottom row stays maxed. The flicker, the rising wisps, the fade — all of it falls out of four lines and a good palette.

Three forces per agent: separate from the close, align with the near, steer toward the average. No central coordinator, no flock state — coherent flocking emerges from local rules.

003 Verlet 1967 ≈ 25 lines

Verlet chain

Each particle remembers only its current and previous position; velocity is implicit in the difference. Constraints become projections — clamp the distance, let the next frame catch up. The math is older than calculus.

Perlin flow field

A 2D noise field, each value reinterpreted as an angle. Particles drift along the local flow direction, leaving trails. The noise stays the same; the particles trace it for you.

005 Fournier · Fussell · Carpenter 1982 ≈ 30 lines
129 × 129 heightmap Generating
recursive midpoint subdivision

Diamond square

Start with four corner heights. Set the midpoint to their average plus a random jitter. Recurse, halving the jitter each level. Out pops a believable mountain range — fractal terrain in a couple dozen lines.

006 Nintendo · SNES 1990 ≈ 25 lines One divide per scanline
360 × 154 Cruising
perspective-mapped texture F-Zero, Mario Kart

Mode 7

A perspective ground plane on hardware that couldn't do 3D. For each scanline of the floor, one division gives you world depth at that row; sample the texture along it linearly. F-Zero, Mario Kart, every fake racing game of the 16-bit era.

007 Spatial subdivision 1980s ≈ 30 lines
320 balls · O(N) Colliding
grid-bucket broad phase

Spatial hash

Hash each object's position into a grid cell; only test pairs that share a cell. Three hundred bouncing balls become O(N) instead of O(N²). The same trick scales to particle systems and character collisions.

Click anywhere to push the balls.

008 Damped harmonic oscillator ≈ 10 lines
linear vs critically damped Tracking
lerp arrives flat · spring arrives soft

Critically damped spring

Linear interpolation overshoots its target on impulse and feels artificial. A critically damped spring — closed-form, ten lines — never overshoots and always arrives. Once you feel the difference, you'll never use lerp for camera follow again.

Move the cursor to compare.

009 Frisken · Quilez et al. 1990s–2000s ≈ 20 lines Implicit geometry
distance field · smin blend Morphing
no triangles · no meshes · pure math

Signed distance blobs

Define each shape by a function returning its distance to any point. Compose with min for hard unions, with a soft variant for liquid blends. Render by reading the field. The same idea, in three dimensions on a GPU, draws Inigo Quilez's entire universe.

Move the cursor to attract a blob.

010 Stam 1999 ≈ 80 lines Stable Navier–Stokes
96 × 96 grid Mixing
semi-Lagrangian advection · pressure projection 20 Jacobi iterations

Stable fluids

Real-time Navier–Stokes that won't blow up. Instead of marching the velocity field forward through space (which is unstable), trace it backward — for each cell, ask "where did this fluid come from one timestep ago?" and sample there. The whole solver fits on two pages and remains stable at any timestep.

Drag the canvas to push the fluid around.

011 Bridson 2007 ≈ 30 lines
100 streamers · 32-point trails Flowing
noise → potential → curl → motion

Curl noise

Sample 2D Perlin noise as a scalar potential and take its curl analytically. The result is a divergence-free vector field — particles flowing through it behave like an incompressible fluid, no solver required. The same trick that drew the smoke in How to Train Your Dragon.

Fluid on the GPU

Stam's stable fluids algorithm, but every step lives in a fragment shader. Velocity, dye, and pressure all stored in floating-point textures; each simulation pass is a full-screen quad rendered to a framebuffer. Same math as entry 010, run on hardware that chews through millions of cells per frame.

Tricks you can feel but not see

Some of the most beautiful optimizations don't show up on a canvas — they show up in a frame budget. A few worth knowing about even though they refuse to be drawn.

Fast inverse square root

Reinterpret a float as an integer, subtract from 0x5f3759df, shift right by one, refine once with Newton-Raphson. Two lines, ~4× faster than the math library, accurate to 1%. Quake III shipped it in 1999.

Quaternions for rotation

Four numbers instead of nine. Compose rotations with 16 multiplies instead of 27, interpolate smoothly with slerp, and dodge gimbal lock entirely. Every 3D engine uses them under the hood for cameras and skeletal animation.

Bilinear filtering as a free lerp

GPUs interpolate texture samples in hardware. Encode anything — a lookup table, a height field, a gradient — into a texture, sample between texels, and you've got linear interpolation for the cost of a memory read.

Bresenham's line algorithm

Draw a line using only integer addition and comparison — no floats, no division. The same family gives you midpoint circles and antialiased curves. Quietly still shipping in plotters, embedded displays, and pixel-art tools.
