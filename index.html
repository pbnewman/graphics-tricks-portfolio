The Doom fire effect

Running

Tap the fire to change its color

A handful of rules. A very convincing inferno.

How it works +
  1. Heat the bottom row
  2. Carry heat upward
  3. Jitter and cool
  4. Look up a color

Each pixel reads from the row below with random horizontal jitter; a coin flip dims it by one palette index. Bottom row stays maxed. The flicker, the rising wisps, the fade — all of it falls out of four lines and a good palette.

024 Floyd & Steinberg 1976 ≈ 50 lines Error diffusion

Floyd–Steinberg dither

Quantizing

Move or drag to add light · tap to change levels

A beautiful image made by carefully distributing its mistakes.

How it works +
  1. Round a pixel to a palette color
  2. Pass the error to its neighbors
  3. Continue across the image

For each pixel, round it to the nearest available palette entry — then spread the rounding error across its right and bottom-row neighbors in the famous 7/16, 3/16, 5/16, 1/16 weights. The mistakes cancel each other out across the image; only at any one pixel does the lie show. Half a page of pseudo-code that gave every 8-bit display its own visual grammar. 025 id Software · Carmack 1992 ≈ 80 lines One ray per column

Ray casting

Walking

Focus the canvas and use WASD or arrows to drive

A flat map becomes a world you can walk through.

How it works +
  1. Cast a ray for each column
  2. Find the first wall
  3. Turn its distance into height

A 3D-feeling world rendered without any 3D math. The map is a 2D grid; for each column of the screen, fire a ray and DDA-step through the grid until you hit a wall. The distance to that hit becomes the wall slice's height — closer is taller, farther is shorter — and a fish-eye correction stops the edges from bowing. Two darknesses for the two wall orientations give you shading for free. The trick that birthed Wolfenstein 3D, Doom, and every first-person shooter that followed.

028 NovaLogic · Comanche 1992 ≈ 40 lines Heightmap raymarch

Voxel space

Cruising

Move or drag sideways to look around

Rolling terrain, painted one screen column at a time.

How it works +
  1. Walk into a heightmap
  2. Project each height
  3. Keep the nearest visible surface

No voxels, despite the name. For each screen column, walk forward from the camera; at each step sample a heightmap, project the height to a screen y, and fill the strip from there down to whatever this column has already drawn. A y-buffer per column handles occlusion automatically — far peaks only paint above near ones. Comanche shipped rolling terrain in 1992 on a 33 MHz CPU with this one trick.

030 Demoscene early 1990s ≈ 30 lines UV lookup table

Tunnel

Falling

Move or drag to drift · tap to change color

An endless descent, made from a single flat texture.

How it works +
  1. Find angle and inverse distance
  2. Scroll through a texture
  3. Look up the color

Every pixel on the screen has a fixed angle to the center and a fixed inverse-distance from it. Compute both at startup, store them in two lookup tables, and you never compute them again. Per frame, just add a time offset to v and sample a texture. The illusion of plunging down an infinite shaft falls out of the fact that pixels near the center have huge inverse-distances and so scroll past faster. All the heavy math happened before the demo started.

018 Vogel 1979 ≈ 15 lines

Phyllotaxis

Packing

Adjust the angle to discover new patterns

The packing trick hiding in a sunflower.

How it works +
  1. Place the next seed farther out
  2. Rotate by a fixed angle
  3. Repeat a thousand times

Place the n-th seed at radius √n and angle n · 137.5°. Any other angle and you get awkward spokes and gaps; the golden angle — an irrational fraction of a turn — is the only rotation no integer ever lands twice on, so nothing ever overlaps. Sunflowers, pinecones, and pineapples all solve this packing problem the same way.

Shedding

Tap to give the flow a kick

A rainbow reveals the vortices hiding behind an obstacle.

How it works +
  1. Store particles in nine directions
  2. Collide and stream
  3. Bounce off obstacles

Instead of solving Navier–Stokes directly, you store at each grid cell a tiny distribution of fictitious particles streaming in nine directions, and apply two rules per tick: collide (relax toward equilibrium) and stream (shift each direction's population by one cell). Tricks you can feel but not see

Some of the most beautiful optimizations don't show up on a canvas — they show up in a frame budget. A few worth knowing about even though they refuse to be drawn.

Fast inverse square root

Reinterpret a float as an integer, subtract from 0x5f3759df, shift right by one, refine once with Newton-Raphson. Two lines, ~4× faster than the math library, accurate to 1%. Quake III shipped it in 1999.

Quaternions for rotation

Four numbers instead of nine. Compose rotations with 16 multiplies instead of 27, interpolate smoothly with slerp, and dodge gimbal lock entirely. Every 3D engine uses them under the hood for cameras and skeletal animation.

Bilinear filtering as a free lerp

GPUs interpolate texture samples in hardware. Encode anything — a lookup table, a height field, a gradient — into a texture, sample between texels, and you've got linear interpolation for the cost of a memory read.

Bresenham's line algorithm

Draw a line using only integer addition and comparison — no floats, no division. The same family gives you midpoint circles and antialiased curves. Quietly still shipping in plotters, embedded displays, and pixel-art tools.

Premultiplied alpha

Store color × alpha together instead of keeping them separate. Compositing collapses to a single multiply-add with no divides, and the dark-fringe halo around alpha-blended sprites simply vanishes. Pixar's RenderMan used it for decades; every <canvas> on the web composites this way and almost nobody notices.

Schlick's Fresnel approximation

The real Fresnel equation involves trig and complex numbers. Christophe Schlick noticed in 1994 that F₀ + (1−F₀)(1−cosθ)⁵ is visually indistinguishable across the angles that matter. One pow, no trig. Every metallic surface in every PBR renderer shipping today runs this line.
