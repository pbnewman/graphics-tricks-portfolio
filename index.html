Critically damped spring

Linear interpolation overshoots its target on impulse and feels artificial. A critically damped spring — closed-form, ten lines — never overshoots and always arrives. Once you feel the difference, you'll never use lerp for camera follow again.

010 Stam 1999 ≈ 80 lines Stable Navier–Stokes
96 × 96 grid Mixing
semi-Lagrangian advection · pressure projection 20 Jacobi iterations

Stable fluids

Real-time Navier–Stokes that won't blow up. Instead of marching the velocity field forward through space (which is unstable), trace it backward — for each cell, ask "where did this fluid come from one timestep ago?" and sample there. The whole solver fits on two pages and remains stable at any timestep.

Sample 2D Perlin noise as a scalar potential and take its curl analytically. The result is a divergence-free vector field — particles flowing through it behave like an incompressible fluid, no solver required. The same trick that drew the smoke in How to Train Your Dragon.

012 Conway 1970 ≈ 70 lines

B3/S23 · toroidal wrap 3 rules · Turing-complete

Conway's Game of Life

Three rules on a square grid: a live cell with two or three live neighbors stays alive, a dead cell with exactly three live neighbors flickers on, everyone else dies. Out of that handful of arithmetic you get gliders, pulsars, glider guns, an entire ecology of patterns — and a proof that this two-state grid is Turing-complete. The original demonstration that complexity is just simplicity compounded.

016 Witten & Sander 1981 ≈ 60 lines Stochastic growth

Diffusion-limited aggregation

A single sticky seed at the center. Random walkers spawn just outside the cluster and Brownian-walk until they touch a fixed particle — whereupon they freeze. Out of pure noise grows the universal dendrite: the silhouette of frost on glass, copper electrodeposition, lightning in a Lichtenberg block, coral. The fingers grow because the tips reach incoming walkers first — a runaway that's literally called the Mullins–Sekerka instability.

018 Vogel 1979 ≈ 15 lines
1200 seeds Packing
angle = n · 137.5077°

Phyllotaxis

Place the n-th seed at radius √n and angle n · 137.5°. Any other angle and you get awkward spokes and gaps; the golden angle — an irrational fraction of a turn — is the only rotation no integer ever lands twice on, so nothing ever overlaps. Sunflowers, pinecones, and pineapples all solve this packing problem the same way.

019 Gumin 2016 ≈ 130 lines Constraint propagation
70 × 30 grid · 16-tile alphabet Collapsing
min-entropy heuristic · edge-match constraints

Wave function collapse

Every cell starts holding all sixteen possible tiles superposed. Find the cell with the fewest remaining options, randomly pick one of them, then propagate the consequences: any neighbor still holding a tile whose edge doesn't match must drop it — and those drops cascade outward in turn. The Sudoku trick, weaponized for procedural worlds. You can watch the certainty wave radiate across the grid in real time.

020 McNamara & Zanetti 1988 ≈ 130 lines Lattice fluid
D2Q9 lattice · 280 × 80 Shedding
collide · stream · bounce-back Re ≈ 140 · 8k tracer particles

Lattice Boltzmann

Instead of solving Navier–Stokes directly, you store at each grid cell a tiny distribution of fictitious particles streaming in nine directions, and apply two rules per tick: collide (relax toward equilibrium) and stream (shift each direction's population by one cell). Add two obstacles in the channel, seed thousands of massless tracers tagged by their starting height, and the Kármán vortex street paints itself in rainbow — each color a streamline, each wiggle the periodic shedding that trails every smokestack and lighthouse at the same lazy rhythm regardless of scale.

022 Lorensen & Cline 1987 ≈ 60 lines Contour extraction

16-case lookup table

Marching squares

Sample a scalar field at the corners of a grid. For each cell, look at which of the four corners are above the threshold — sixteen possible patterns — and emit the contour segment connecting the appropriate edge midpoints. Linearly interpolate where it crosses the threshold for smoothness. The 2D ancestor of marching cubes, and how every contour line on every topographic map was ever drawn.

Voronoi + Lloyd

Drop seeds on a plane. Color every point by which seed is closest. The diagram that falls out is everywhere — cell biology, crystallography, mobile coverage maps. Lloyd's contribution: each step, move every seed slightly toward the centroid of its own cell, and repeat. The seeds drift apart until they fill space like soap bubbles, settling into the blue-noise distribution used by everything from image stippling to dart-throwing samplers to procedural terrain.

024 Floyd & Steinberg 1976 ≈ 50 lines Error diffusion

kernel: 7/16 · 3/16 · 5/16 · 1/16 2 / 4 / 8 / 16 levels

Floyd–Steinberg dither

For each pixel, round it to the nearest available palette entry — then spread the rounding error across its right and bottom-row neighbors in the famous 7/16, 3/16, 5/16, 1/16 weights. The mistakes cancel each other out across the image; only at any one pixel does the lie show. Half a page of pseudo-code that gave every 8-bit display its own visual grammar. 025 id Software · Carmack 1992 ≈ 80 lines One ray per column
120 rays · 20 × 16 grid map Walking
DDA traversal · fish-eye corrected Wolfenstein 3D

Ray casting

A 3D-feeling world rendered without any 3D math. The map is a 2D grid; for each column of the screen, fire a ray and DDA-step through the grid until you hit a wall. The distance to that hit becomes the wall slice's height — closer is taller, farther is shorter — and a fish-eye correction stops the edges from bowing. Two darknesses for the two wall orientations give you shading for free. The trick that birthed Wolfenstein 3D, Doom, and every first-person shooter that followed.

Mandelbrot & Julia

The same four-line iteration z ← z² + c on the complex plane draws two of the most famous shapes in mathematics. Tricks you can feel but not see

Some of the most beautiful optimizations don't show up on a canvas — they show up in a frame budget. A few worth knowing about even though they refuse to be drawn.

Fast inverse square root

Reinterpret a float as an integer, subtract from 0x5f3759df, shift right by one, refine once with Newton-Raphson. Two lines, ~4× faster than the math library, accurate to 1%. Quake III shipped it in 1999.

Quaternions for rotation

Four numbers instead of nine. Compose rotations with 16 multiplies instead of 27, interpolate smoothly with slerp, and dodge gimbal lock entirely. Every 3D engine uses them under the hood for cameras and skeletal animation.

Bilinear filtering as a free lerp

GPUs interpolate texture samples in hardware. Encode anything — a lookup table, a height field, a gradient — into a texture, sample between texels, and you've got linear interpolation for the cost of a memory read.

Bresenham's line algorithm

Draw a line using only integer addition and comparison — no floats, no division. The same family gives you midpoint circles and antialiased curves. Quietly still shipping in plotters, embedded displays, and pixel-art tools.

Premultiplied alpha

Store color × alpha together instead of keeping them separate. Compositing collapses to a single multiply-add with no divides, and the dark-fringe halo around alpha-blended sprites simply vanishes. Pixar's RenderMan used it for decades; every <canvas> on the web composites this way and almost nobody notices.

Schlick's Fresnel approximation

The real Fresnel equation involves trig and complex numbers. Christophe Schlick noticed in 1994 that F₀ + (1−F₀)(1−cosθ)⁵ is visually indistinguishable across the angles that matter. One pow, no trig. Every metallic surface in every PBR renderer shipping today runs this line.
